Chapter 3

The rain had not stopped since Tuesday. Mr. Hollis stood at the window of the old post office on St. Mary's Road, watching the lamps flicker along Dr. Pell's garden wall.

"You should go home," said Mrs. Hollis. "It is nearly nine o'clock."

"In a minute." He did not turn around. The letter in his hand had been opened and folded and opened again. Each time, it said the same thing: the estate would be sold by the end of the month, unless someone paid the arrears.

"Richard, please." She crossed the room and stopped beside him. "Whatever it says, it is only paper."

"Paper." He laughed, but there was no humour in it. "The Ministry does not send paper, Ellen. It sends people."

The inventory listed 3.14 tons of coal, two lamps, e.g. the brass ones, and a cart, i.e. the one with the broken wheel, etc. None of it was worth much.

J. R. Okafor, the solicitor, had written the letter himself. See www.example.org/estate-notes. He had underlined the last line twice.

"I... I don't know," she said. "Wait... maybe the bank will listen."

The U.S. consultant, a quiet man from Boston, arrived at ten. He said nothing. Then he left.

Yes. Nothing else happened that night.
